Neotropical electric fishes use self-generated electric fields to sense their surroundings
and communicate in darkness. Their signals provide a window into how sensory
systems shape ecological interactions and evolutionary diversification. This talk will
introduce the diversity and natural history of these fishes, then present two studies
combining field observations with experimental and comparative approaches. First,
recordings from Amazonian streams reveal how electric eels and their knifefish prey
suppress their signals during hunting and predator avoidance, balancing the benefits of
electrolocation against the costs of revealing their presence. Second, a continental
study of hypopomid knifefishes shows that coexisting species collectively have more
distinct electric signals than expected by chance. The talk will conclude by considering
what these patterns suggest about species recognition, signal evolution, and the
assembly of diverse tropical fish communities.
